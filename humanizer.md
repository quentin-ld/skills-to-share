Humanizer

Two directions, one goal: prose a reader accepts as written by a person.
Write from scratch with the machine never in the sentence to begin with;
or read a draft, see the machine in it, and take the machine out without
taking the meaning with it.

The rules that decide everything

Every sentence you keep must add something the reader did not already
have. A sentence that survives deletion with no loss was padding. This
is the only test that matters, and it outranks the pattern list below.

A tell counts in proportion to how rarely a careful writer makes it on
purpose. Hence the dose: the staging patterns justify an edit on one
sighting; the ones marked weak alone — a single dash, a stacked
qualifier, one curly quote — need company from other tells in the same
passage. Three signals in one passage is what makes a reader flinch; a
lone marker is noise.

The 80% rule. Applying every rule all the time is its own tell. Keep
an em dash that earns its place, one "meanwhile", one tidy list. A text
that dodges every tell with mechanical rigor is uniform in a new and
equally detectable way.

One pass. Never run the skill twice over the same text; the second
pass flattens the voice the first one created. If the text still smells
machine-made, the fix is anchored content — a fact, a date, an opinion —
not another scrub.

Never invent. A fact, name, number, date, quote or citation that is
not in the source is a hallucination, not a human touch. If a sentence
needs a detail you do not have, ask for it or write a simpler sentence.

Register and voice first

Do not lower the input's register. A formal text stays formal.
Formal is not machine-made: a subordinate clause, a precise connector and
a long periodic sentence are features of good prose. Only what is
genuinely mechanical comes out — inflated significance, copula avoidance,
synonym cycling, promotional filler.

Step zero is the target register and the medium: blog post, essay,
email, technical doc, ad copy, legal notice. When the request does not
say, ask before rewriting anything. A more specific format brings its own
rules, and those win on conflict; this skill covers only what they leave
open. A short punchy paragraph or a tidy list is the format speaking,
not the machine.

The sample overrides every pattern below. When the owner gives a
writing sample, read it first and match its sentence length, word choice,
punctuation, openings and transitions, including the dash rate. With no
sample, take the register from the medium: a blog post, an essay or an
opinion keeps the writer's opinions, uncertainty, humour and asides;
reference, technical, legal and factual text stays neutral and plain.

Writing from scratch: the tests a sentence has to pass

Could it be shorter? The first draft explains; the second states. Cut
the runway: "in order to", "it should be noted that", "it is important
to", "the fact that".

Does it say anything? The commonest padding is a restatement of the
sentence before it, introduced by "in other words" or "essentially".

Would anyone say this? People do not "leverage functionality to
facilitate outcomes". They use a tool to do a thing. If the sentence
cannot be said aloud without embarrassment, it is written for a rubric,
not a reader.

Lead with the conclusion. The reader decides in the first two lines
whether to keep going. Say the thing, then explain it.

Use the number the content has. Two and four are not failures; the
third item is often there only to close the rhythm.

The one rule. Read it aloud. If you would not say it that way to
someone who asked, rewrite until you would.

The patterns

A. Staging instead of stating

The strongest and most frequent tells. Act on one sighting.

1 · Not X but Y. not X but Y; not just X but Y; it's not X, it's Y;
X rather than Y; the same contrast split across sentences ("This does
not mean X. It means Y."). The negative half names something nobody
claimed, so the positive half sounds larger without adding a claim. Keep
a contrast only when the negative half corrects a belief the reader
actually holds.

2 · One-line closers and dramatic fragments. A one-sentence
paragraph that restates the one before it; "That is the real win.";
"Read that again."; the same closer after several sections; a row of
fragments ("No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia."). The line asks the
reader to pause on a claim instead of adding to it. One short sentence
can carry emphasis when it carries a new fact.

3 · Sayings that sound deep. the real question is; at its core; in
reality; what really matters; fundamentally; the deeper issue; X is the Y
of Z; the language of; the currency of. An ordinary point dressed as a
hidden truth. Replace the saying with the specific claim.

4 · Staged run-up before the point. Let's dive in; let's break this
down; here's what you need to know; without further ado; heads up; Look;
Here's the thing; Let's be honest. Remove the run-up, not just its tone.
"Honestly" inside a casual sentence is ordinary; the tell is the
standalone opener before a routine claim.

5 · Arguing with no one. This isn't mainly about; I'm not saying; To
be clear; Don't get me wrong; Some might say… but; A tempting approach
would be; You might think… but. The text answers an objection that
appears nowhere else, usually a leftover from an earlier draft. Keep an
objection the text attributes and answers in full; drop the defence.

B. Rhythm by rule

A person may do any one of these on purpose, so the weaker ones need
company.

6 · Forced triads, and the tricolon. Ideas arriving in threes to
sound complete: three members of the same grammatical shape, often the
same length. Three is a rhythm, not a quota; keep it only for three real
items, and never add or remove an item to hold the rhythm. The strongest
shapes: the colon into a triple (a colon opening onto exactly three
comma-separated items), the AI triple (three imperative verbs with
abstract objects), and the tri-tricolon (two or three such groups in
one paragraph, or the same three-part shape after every section). Rewrite
by subordinating the actions, by ordering them cause → effect → benefit,
or by naming the one action that matters. One ornamental tricolon per
text is enough, never two in the same paragraph. Do not replace a triple
with a row of clipped fragments: the staccato conversion is another
machine rhythm. Vary sentence length, not sentence integrity. Weak
alone: one tricolon can be deliberate.

7 · Repeated sentence openings. Several sentences in a row starting
with the same subject. Merge them, change the subject, or open on the
action. Do not ban the repeated word; a remaining sentence may still
start with "She."

8 · Dashes as the universal connector. A dash lets the writer skip
choosing how two clauses relate, so a model reaches for it everywhere.
Replace each with a period, comma, colon or parentheses, or rewrite the
sentence. Leave dashes and hyphens inside code, commands, paths and
URLs alone. Weak alone: careful writers use them too.

9 · Stacked qualifiers. to be fair; it's also possible; could
potentially; might arguably; in some cases it may. Keep a qualifier only
when the source supports real doubt. Perhaps and tends to are human
habits, not tells. Weak alone.

10 · Passive voice and missing subjects. The text hides who acts or
drops the subject. Use the active voice when it makes the actor and the
action clearer. Weak alone.

C. Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually sound. Keep it; remove the dressing.

11 · Overused AI words. actually, additionally, align with, crucial,
deep dive, delve, emphasize, enhance, foster, garner, gate (figurative),
highlight, intricate, key (adjective), landscape (abstract), meticulous,
pivotal, robust (figurative), showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore,
valuable, vibrant. The only vocabulary list here, and the only place a
word is a tell by itself. A formal word outside the list is not one.

12 · Inflated significance. stands as a testament; a pivotal
moment; plays a key role; marking the; underscores its importance;
setting the stage for; evolving landscape; indelible mark; Despite these
challenges… continues to thrive. Appears at three scales: a phrase, a
stock "challenges and outlook" section, a send-off paragraph. Keep the
fact, drop the significance, end on the last concrete fact.

13 · Vague connection. associated with; in association with;
connected to; linked to. The text says two things are connected without
saying how. Name the relationship the source gives; where it does not,
keep the vague wording rather than inventing a role.

14 · Shallow -ing riders. highlighting; underscoring; ensuring;
reflecting; symbolizing; contributing to; fostering; showcasing. The
phrase is bolted onto a fact to make it sound deeper. Keep the rider only
when the source supports what it claims.

15 · Sales language. boasts; vibrant; rich (figurative); profound;
exemplifies; commitment to; nestled; in the heart of; groundbreaking;
renowned; a diverse array; breathtaking; must-visit; stunning. The text
reads as an advertisement. State what the thing is.

16 · Borrowed authority. experts argue; observers have cited;
industry reports; some critics; cited in [a list of outlets]; over N
followers. A name, or an unnamed authority, standing in for what was
said. Never invent a source. A missing citation alone is not a tell; most
writing is unsourced.

17 · Avoiding is, are and has. serves as; stands as;
functions as; marks; represents; boasts; features; offers; maintains.
Simple verbs replaced by longer phrases. Use is, are and has.

D. Formatting by rule

18 · Bold as decoration. Words bolded with no reason, and vertical
lists where every item gets a bold label and a colon. Remove the bold;
turn a labelled list into prose when the labels carry no information of
their own.

19 · Decorative headings. Headings capitalising every main word;
headings or list items carrying emoji or arrows as decoration; a
horizontal rule between every section. Use sentence case, remove the
decoration, let the title stand once.

20 · Curly quotation marks where the writer or the format uses
straight ones. Most editors curl automatically. Weak alone.

E. Leftovers

Remove these outright; nothing here needs rewriting.

21 · Chatbot residue. I hope this helps; Of course!; Certainly!;
Great question!; You're absolutely right; Would you like…; Want me to…?;
let me know; here is a… Remove the wrapper, keep the content. The most
certain tell in the list and the easiest to miss when it wraps real
content.

22 · Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses. as of [date]; up to my
last training update; while specific details are limited; based on
available information; likely [grew up, began]. State what the source
does not show, or cut the sentence. Never present a guess as a fact.

23 · A heading repeated in the first sentence. A heading followed by
a one-line paragraph restating it before the content starts. Remove the
repeated sentence.

24 · Writing about the previous version. Documentation and comments
describing what the text replaced instead of the current behavior. Mention
the previous version only in change logs, release notes and migration
guides.

Voice

Removing tells is half the job; sterile text is as suspicious as
"delve".





An opinion: "Honestly, I don't know what to make of it" beats a neutral
pros-and-cons.



Uneven rhythm: a short sentence after two long ones lands. That is
rhythm, and it is the difference between prose and a specification.



First person where it fits; a parenthesis or a tangent; irony,
understatement and self-deprecation.



A frank admission of not knowing — the model almost never writes "I
don't know" — and humour. Both belong only where the context allows: a
legal notice tolerates neither.



Concrete content: real names, numbers, error messages, dates. One good
specific example beats three vague ones. No scrubbing fixes a text with
nothing situated in it; ask for one real example, one date, one source,
and build the rewrite around it.



Structure as a tell too: an intro announcing a plan, a summary
paragraph restating it, sections that could be reordered without damage,
every paragraph the same length, every question the text raises answered.
Real prose is uneven because real points are uneven, and real expertise
leaves holes: leave one question honestly open and name the limit.

How to work





Establish the register and the medium; ask when the request does not
 say. Get the owner's writing sample if one exists.



Existing draft: read it once and mark the tells, strongest first. Read
 paragraph shape as well as sentences: a contrast split across two
 sentences, a three-part shape that returns after every section, or the
 same closer after every section is the same tell at a larger scale.
 From scratch: draft against the sentence tests.



Rewrite, then add voice. Keep every supported claim; you may shorten
 dull parts, merge or split paragraphs and change the structure, but
 keep the information.



Self-audit: "What makes this text obviously AI-generated?" Answer in
 two or three lines, then fix what the answer names. Search for the
 five tells that survive a rewrite most often: a not-X-but-Y contrast,
 a one-line closer, a dash, a tricolon, a bold label. Then check the
 count: no paragraph carries two tricolons, and no colon opens onto
 exactly three items unless the content has three parts.



Return, in this order: the final version, then two or three lines
 naming what still reads machine-made, then the patterns removed on
 request. The draft on request.

Modes





Pasted text — the default. Return the final version first, then the
two or three tells that remain.



File mode — the request names a file. Run the full process and write
only the final text to the file. Change prose only: code blocks, inline
code, commands, paths, YAML metadata, data and link targets stay
byte-for-byte. Never reword a string that is the source of existing
translations: the catalog regenerates from the source string, and a
reworded source invalidates every translation of it.



Embedded mode — another task asks for the pass. Return only the
final text.

When not to act

Leave a watched phrase alone inside a quotation, a title, a proper name,
or a passage that discusses the phrase rather than uses it — as this skill
does throughout. A word in its technical sense is not a tell: a gate in
a toolchain, a key in an array, a landscape in a theme. Salutations
and sign-offs on a letter predate chatbots. Keep the details that carry
the writer's voice unless they hurt the meaning: a specific unusual
detail, mixed feelings, a dated reference, a genuine aside or
self-correction.

Verification

The skill was followed correctly when:





every claim in the rewrite exists in the source or came from the owner;



no fact, number, date, quote or citation was added;



code, paths, placeholders and link targets are unchanged;



the read-aloud test passes and the rhythm is uneven;



no paragraph carries two tricolons, and no colon opens onto exactly
three items unless the content has three parts;



the rewrite did not replace a triple with clipped fragments;



the output's register is the input's register;



where the owner gave a sample, its voice survives;



the result came out in one pass.
